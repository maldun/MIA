# MIA research: a literature review of a research question (requirements, draft 1, 2026-10-21)

Replaces the idea "research = answer one question from a few web pages" (13.1 of `REQUIREMENTS_CORE.md`) as the **main** research task. The old pipelines stay as the quick path (RV3). Builds on 13b (strategies), 13c (context graph), 4a (library), 9 (evaluation), 12 (verification).
How to read it: **Decided** = from the discussion of 2026-10-21; **Proposal** = to be built and measured; **Evidence** = what the literature says, with how well it was checked. Nothing here is built yet.

## 1. Principle: break a problem down level by level (decided)

A small model cannot digest a whole problem, as a person cannot digest a whole PDF at once. So a task is worked **top down in levels**, and each level is done by a narrow role:

- RV1. **Three planning roles** (prompt views of a model, see RV20, not three programs):
  1. **System analyser**: describes the *structure* of the problem first: the concepts, the sub-questions, the fields involved, what kind of evidence would answer it, what is out of scope.
  2. **Component determiner**: takes that structure and names the *components*: which experts (RV10), which keyword sets, which source types.
  3. **Scheduler**: turns the components into the *tasks* in order, each with a budget (RV30) and a target a check can test (13b ST1).
- RV2. **Granularity matches the context window.** A piece of work is only handed to a model if its input fits a fixed share of the model's context (`num_ctx` of the profile, default budget 50 percent, the rest is for instructions and answer). Code counts tokens; if a piece is too big it is split **one level deeper** by the same procedure (sections of a paper, then paragraphs; sub-questions of a sub-question). The model is never asked to decide what fits.
- RV3. **Two tasks, two names (decided by the user, 2026-10-22).** The old research process stays as **lookup**, a weaker form of research that still serves for quick questions: a fact check of one statement, cheap (RV40: first a source whose trust is high enough (RV14), a model only if that is not enough). **Research** is the literature review of a research question (RV4), the main task; a single question is too atomic to be the unit of research, the review is. Chat commands: `/lookup` (the old `/research`, renamed 2026-10-22; `/research` still works as a named alias until the review exists), `/search` as before, and `/research` for the literature review once it is built. The names inside the code (the skill `research`, the pipelines `research.*`, the suites `research` and `research_compound`, the module `research.py`) still carry the old name; they appear in recorded runs and in the baselines of `eval/baselines`, and are renamed to `lookup.*` when the new research is introduced, not before.

## 2. What MIA delivers: the literature review (decided)

- RV4. **Input:** a research question, optional scope (fields, languages, **a date: only literature published up to that date**, default today), a budget (RV30).
- RV5. **Output:** a structured report (RV27) with the sources found and why each was kept or dropped, the findings per sub-question with verbatim anchors, conflicts between sources, and an **answerability** figure in percent (RV23) with open gaps and suggested next keywords. A low figure is a valid result (RV24).
- RV6. **We do not claim to verify the truth** (decided). We judge how well the available literature lets us answer the question, and show what that judgement rests on.

## 3. Evidence: what the literature says, and what it does not (checked 2026-10-21)

Checked through search results and one fetched paper page (Smit et al.); the other papers only as abstracts or search summaries. The numbers must be read in the papers before they are cited in a publication.

- **Smit et al., "Should we be going MAD?" (ICML 2024, arXiv 2311.17371).** Compared Society of Minds (SoM), Multi-Persona, ChatEval, Ensemble Refinement, Self-Consistency, Medprompt (its ensembling parts) and single-agent prompting on 7 datasets with GPT-3.5 (MedQA, PubMedQA, MMLU, CosmosQA, CIAR, GPQA, Chess); GPT-4 and Mixtral 8x7B only on MedQA. Result: **no debate protocol beats the simpler methods reliably**. Medprompt (ensembling, no debate) was best overall and cheaper; SoM was the strongest debate method on MedQA (0.64 vs 0.65 for Medprompt) and showed the largest gain from debate over the first answer; self-consistency was best on MMLU. Debate costs more calls and tokens. The methods are **sensitive to hyperparameters**: Multi-Persona went from worst to best after tuning, and prompting agents to agree with each other X percent of the time ("agreement modulation") gave about 15 percent for Multi-Persona and 5 percent for SoM on a USMLE subset, while for one dataset (CIAR) disagreement helped. Limits stated by the authors: GPT-3.5 through an API; hyperparameters **transferred poorly to Mixtral 8x7B**; the tasks are multiple choice questions, **not literature reviews**.
  *The analysis of debating behaviour* (their Figure 7, MedQA only, no numbers given in the text): SoM has the largest area across four behaviour measures (accuracy of the final consensus, any agent finding the correct answer, how many agents changed their answer, diversity of the first answers). That says SoM is the **richest debate dynamic** of the three debate protocols compared there; it does not say it gives the best final accuracy. In the accuracy table SoM is best or tied on one of the seven datasets (CIAR, tied with self-consistency), below Medprompt on MedQA by 0.01, and clearly below on MMLU, CosmosQA and Chess. The method that profited most from tuning was **Multi-Persona** (agreement modulation at 90 percent), and the strong tuned result is reported for MedQA only, on a subset used for tuning.
  *Consequence:* "Society of Mind is the best approach" is **not** what the paper shows. It is a promising and competitive candidate whose benefit must be measured against an ensemble with the same number of calls on our models and our task (RV33). Roles and debate are built as interchangeable **judgement styles** (12.1), not as the only architecture.
- **Later work on debate (search results and abstracts only, to be read before use):** (1) "Debate or Vote" (NeurIPS 2025, arXiv 2508.17536): across seven benchmarks plain majority voting accounts for most of the gain usually credited to debate; a theoretical argument says debate alone does not raise expected correctness; for Qwen2.5-7B their table has voting about 0.77 against about 0.67 for centralised debate; but **targeted changes of how agents update beliefs (a bias toward correction)** made debate more effective. (2) "The Cost of Consensus" (2026): with 7 to 8 billion parameter models (Qwen2.5-7B, Llama-3.1-8B, Ministral-3-8B) **unguided homogeneous** teams gained nothing from exchanging answers; isolated self-correction was the better trade of cost and accuracy; debate used 2.1 to 3.4 times the tokens; named failures: conformity (sycophancy), fragility to context, collapse of consensus. (3) "Large Language Models Cannot Self-Correct Reasoning Yet" (arXiv 2310.01798): at equal numbers of responses debate was worse than self-consistency by majority vote. *Consequence:* for **small models and unguided, homogeneous agents** the evidence is negative. What is **not** answered by these (as far as two searches showed; not an exhaustive review) is the case we plan: **agents with different roles and different evidence**, grounded by code-checked anchors, on a literature task.
- **Contributing views instead of debating (searched 2026-10-21; abstracts and search summaries only).** The first workflow of the discussion (each expert adds its view, nobody argues) is closer to these than to debate:
  - *STORM* (Shao et al., NAACL 2024): "multi-perspective question asking". The perspectives come from existing articles on similar topics and steer which questions are asked of grounded sources. Against an outline-driven retrieval baseline, more articles were judged well organised (+25 percent absolute) and broad in coverage (+10 percent). The expert editors also found new problems: **source bias carried over and unrelated facts wrongly associated**. Closest published match to our idea; it measures coverage of a written article, not recall of a review's included studies.
  - *Mixture-of-Agents* (arXiv 2406.04692): independent *proposers* answer, an *aggregator* fuses; models gave better answers when shown other models' answers, even weaker ones; proposers should add diverse context. Measured on general answer quality (AlpacaEval), with different models as proposers, not on research recall.
  - *Vocabulary mismatch* (the "vocabulary problem", Furnas et al., classic): the same thing has different names in different fields or even within one; thesauri and ontologies (MeSH, UMLS in biomedicine) are the proven remedy for synonyms; LLM query expansion by prompting gives useful extra terms without training (arXiv 2305.03653), but needs **grounding in trusted sources** against invented terms. *DiscipLink* (arXiv 2408.00447) has an LLM write queries in the jargon of other disciplines to bridge the gap for interdisciplinary search (a human-AI system; its evaluation was not read).
  - *Literature-based discovery* (Swanson, "undiscovered public knowledge"; ARROWSMITH, MOLIERE): findings in separate literatures that only together answer a question; the fish oil and Raynaud's case is the standard example. A 2025 survey notes classic methods only predict pairwise relations between concepts, which is the motivation for LLM-based work.
  - **Not found:** a direct test of "one agent per field proposes terms and views, and this finds answers that exist in another field under another name" with an LLM, measured against a reference set. Also **nothing that refutes it**; the closest warnings are STORM's over-association and the invention of terms by query expansion. Two to four searches per strand, not an exhaustive review.
- **Method transfer is harder than vocabulary (searched 2026-10-21; abstracts and search summaries only).** (1) *Analogy mining* (Hope, Chan, Kittur, Shahaf, KDD 2017 / IJCAI 2018): representing ideas by **purpose and mechanism** found analogies with higher precision and recall than TF-IDF, LSA, LDA and GloVe retrieval, and analogies shown to people raised the creativity of their ideas; the experiments used product descriptions, not scientific papers. (2) *SCAR* (400 scientific analogies from 13 fields): ChatGPT and GPT-4 struggled to abduce the shared relational structure of two systems; a 2025 study finds LLM analogy errors come from surface matching; *YARN* (2026) reports that having the model first decompose and **abstract** the units before mapping improves results. (3) *IsoSci* (2026) pairs isomorphic problems from different sciences to separate reasoning from knowledge retrieval; *IDRBench* (arXiv 2507.15736) is an expert-annotated benchmark for identifying interdisciplinary papers and recommending ideas from other domains, the closest existing benchmark for our purpose. *Consequence:* retrieval by words finds the wrong things; **structure has to be made explicit first** (an abstraction step), and the transfer step is where small models are expected to be weakest. No benchmark for "which method of another field solves this problem" as such was found.
- **Diversity of problem solvers (Hong and Page 2004; contested).** In an agent-based model plus a theorem, a random group from a diverse pool beat a group of the best individual performers, because the best performers become similar in how they solve problems [Hong and Page, PNAS 101(46), 16385-16389; search summary only]. The result is disputed: Thompson (Notices of the AMS 2014) argues the mathematics does not carry the strong claims and that the randomness of group formation, not diversity, explains it; Singer (Philosophy of Science 2019) defends the diversity reading; Grim and colleagues (Philosophy of Science 2019) show it depends on the landscape: on smoother landscapes ability beats diversity. *Consequence:* the case for different roles is that top agents of the same kind become alike, so what counts is **functional diversity** (different roles, evidence, tools), and whether it helps is measured with the control "N copies of the best role" against "N different roles" at the same call budget (RV33). Humans are the evidence here; for language models it is a hypothesis.
- **PaperQA2 (FutureHouse, arXiv 2409.13740).** Agent with retrieval, summaries and answer steps; reports expert-level results on retrieval, summarisation and contradiction detection (70 percent of 2.34 contradictions per paper confirmed by experts) and the benchmark LitQA2. Search summaries only; part of the coverage is promotional.
- **OpenScholar / ScholarQABench (arXiv 2411.14199).** Retrieval-augmented LM for scientific questions, a benchmark of 2,967 expert-written queries and 208 long-form answers across computer science, physics, neuroscience, biomedicine; data and code released. A candidate for part of the benchmark (RV31). Abstract and summaries read.
- **LLM abstract screening for systematic reviews** (OLIVER, arXiv 2512.20022; a poster on local open-weight models; a psychiatry study; summaries only): recall depends strongly on review, prompt and model; most models are specific but miss true includes; two-model actor-critic improved discrimination and calibration but sensitivity only a little; a 5-model ensemble reached about 97 percent recall with about 54 percent precision; two broad-scope reviews never reached 95 percent recall with any model; a local Gemma beat a cloud model in one of five reviews. *Consequence:* screening is **recall-oriented**, uses several votes, keeps borderline papers for a second look (RV17), and its recall is measured against reference reviews (RV31).
- **Scholarly metadata:** OpenAlex, Semantic Scholar and Crossref offer free APIs; OpenAlex draws on Crossref. A 2024 study found OpenAlex's single `is_retracted` flag misclassified corrections and expressions of concern as retractions for part of the data; Crossref (which took over the Retraction Watch data) has finer status information, partly in an experimental API. *Consequence:* retraction status is read from Crossref and cross-checked, not taken from one flag (RV15).
- Not checked, from memory: OpenAlex's topic taxonomy (domains, fields, subfields, topics) as a starting point for the field graph (RV11).

> Note (2026-10-22): the variants below are **workflow graphs of one model** (`REQUIREMENTS_CORE.md` 4b): the roles are participants with interfaces and an orchestrator schedules them. Reading debate as an iterative coupling adds variants to test: parallel (Jacobi) against sequential (Gauss-Seidel) rounds, **agreement modulation as a relaxation factor** that is tuned instead of written into a prompt, and a **convergence criterion** that stops the rounds instead of a fixed number. These are hypotheses from an analogy, not findings.

### The research question behind the roles (decided 2026-10-21, as hypotheses to falsify)

H0 (comes first, no debate needed): *Experts of related fields, each proposing the terms and views of their own field, find relevant sources that a single model would miss, especially sources that answer the question under another name in another field.* The same holds one level deeper for **methods** (H0b below): the difference between fields is often not the name but a method the other field has used for a long time. This is the "contribute views" workflow. It has the better support (STORM, query expansion, the vocabulary problem, literature-based discovery), it is cheap to measure, and it does not depend on debate. It is tested **before** H1.

H1 (only if H0 holds): *Small models, given different roles and different evidence (each expert sees the material of its own field) and grounded by code-checked anchors, reach better conclusions when their judgements are combined through a tuned debate than through plain aggregation (voting) of independent role judgements, and the gain is worth the extra cost.* The hope behind it is that tuned debate makes specialised, efficient agents out of small models. The literature above makes that **plausible only in a narrower form** than "debate is powerful when tuned": with identical agents and the same information, debate mostly adds consistency that voting gives cheaper. Different information per agent is the part that can change that, because then the agents are not resampling the same question. H1 is therefore tested as a **ladder of variants where each step must earn its cost** (RV33), and a negative result (aggregation of roles is as good as debate) is a useful result that keeps the cheaper method.

### Transferred solutions: not only names, also methods (decided 2026-10-21 as a goal; the design is a proposal)

Bridging vocabulary (RV41) is the easy part. The valuable part is **a method that one field has used for a long time and another does not know**, where the problem is the same in structure but looks different. Examples named in the discussion (facts from memory, to be verified with sources before they appear in a report or paper): (1) a problem written as continuous optimisation that is a linear programme whose constraint matrix has network structure, so it can be solved as a **flow problem** by network methods, far faster; (2) **"squaring the square"** (dissecting a square into unequal squares), solved in 1940 by Brooks, Smith, Stone and Tutte through **electrical network** theory (Kirchhoff's laws); (3) **Riemannian geometry** taken over to describe general relativity; (4) **PageRank** as the stationary distribution of a Markov chain / principal eigenvector, a method known in mathematics and in bibliometrics (eigenvector ranking, influence weights, Katz centrality) decades before web search used it. These are the standard cases of *transferred solutions*.

H0b: *An expert role that restates a problem by its abstract structure and asks which fields have solved that structure finds transferable methods that a keyword search in the question's own field does not.*

- RV43. **Problem signature.** An analyser step (code-checked schema) restates the question or sub-question as a **structure, without the field's words**: the kind of objects (graph, linear system, Markov chain, matrix, differential equation, order relation, ...), the relations and constraints, the objective (ranking, optimising, deciding, counting, classifying), the scale and the symmetries. The purpose-and-mechanism form of analogy mining is the model for it. Retrieval of analogous problems uses the signature (and the signature's words in other fields' vocabularies, RV41), not the question's text.
- RV44. **A transfer scout role.** It proposes **candidate transfers**: "this has the structure of X; field F solves X with method M". Each candidate carries: the structural mapping (which part of our problem corresponds to which part of X), the sources describing M for X (anchored), the assumptions under which M applies, and what would show that it does not. A candidate is a **hypothesis, never a finding**; the report shows it in its own part, ranked, with a small number of entries (default 3), because most analogies are not useful and a long list is noise.
- RV45. **Test by code where possible.** Many transfers can be checked by computation in the sandbox: whether a constraint matrix has the network structure (code), whether a ranking equals the principal eigenvector of the matrix (numerically on a toy instance), whether method M gives the same answer as the known method on a small instance and how long each takes. A candidate with a passing executable test is marked as **verified on a toy instance**; the others are marked as **proposed by analogy only**. The test is written by a model but judged by code (12.2 hard checks).
- RV46. **Surface similarity is the main failure** (SCAR, the strategic-decision study): the scout must not rank candidates by how alike the words are; the mapping has to name matched parts, and a mapping that only matches words is dropped by a check (the matched parts must have different names in the two fields).
- RV47. **Evaluation by retro-discovery (the date cut-off of RV32 used as designed).** A set of historical transfers with known ground truth (cases like the four above and more from the "transferred solutions" literature). For each: the problem as it stood before the transfer, in the words of the original field, and the allowed literature only up to the year before the transfer; question: does the system propose the method from the other field, in the top 3? Measures: hit rate, rank, false candidates per case, whether the executable test passes, calls and seconds. **Limit that cannot be removed:** the famous cases are in the training data of the models, so a hit on them proves little (memorisation). Therefore: use less famous cases and cases from the last years (after the models' cut-off), keep the famous ones only as sanity checks, and say so in every result. Without this the benchmark would flatter the method.
- RV49. **A catalogue of transfer templates instead of free invention** (proposal of 2026-10-21, to reduce the risk of the abstraction step). Many transfers are known from history and best practice. A **template** is a curated record: *name* (e.g. "linear programme with network structure -> network flow"); the *signature pattern* (RV43) that triggers it; an *applicability test*, **in code where the structure allows it** (is the constraint matrix a network matrix, is the operator self-adjoint, is the signal band-limited, is the system a Markov chain); the *method* and the steps to adapt it; its *preconditions and typical pitfalls*; *sources* (anchored); the *fields* it connects; a *status* (draft, tested, active, as every library entry, 4a); and its *record*. The scout's job then becomes **selecting and adapting** (a classification problem with a short list of known options, which small models handle far better than open invention) instead of inventing. This is case-based reasoning (retrieve, reuse, revise, retain) and has precedents in design catalogues (e.g. TRIZ-like principle lists, algorithm design catalogues, the reductions between problems of complexity theory; not checked here). Templates are data in the library, versioned, readable and editable by the user.
- RV50. **Two tiers, as 13b ST5 already decided for plans.** Tier 1 = the catalogue (rigid, trusted after tests). Tier 2 = the free scout (RV44), off by default, whose candidates run as **challengers** next to the templates and are promoted to a new template only after they were verified (RV45) and proved useful over enough cases, otherwise kept as a logged negative example. The catalogue limits what the scout can do wrong; the challenger tier is how the catalogue grows. A catalogue cannot find a transfer nobody has written down: that limit is accepted and stated.
- RV51. **A record per template and problem class, in the context graph** (13c CG7 decision traces). Each use of a template stores: the problem signature class, the template and its version, the adaptation, whether the executable test passed, whether the user kept the suggestion, calls and seconds. The statistics (hits, passes, kept) per template and per class are what the selection learns from (a contextual bandit over templates, 8 of the core requirements, with the ladder rule: defaults first, learned choices logged beside the default, all switchable off). With few uses per user the statistics are thin, so sample sizes and intervals are shown and a template is never dropped by a handful of cases.
- RV52. **Where templates come from.** Start by hand in one domain where the user has expertise (the examples below are applied mathematics: optimisation, signals, numerical analysis, algebra), so that the catalogue can be judged by an expert and the first tests are meaningful. Later sources: surveys and textbooks (with anchors), the literature-based-discovery work, and promoted challengers (RV50). A model may *draft* a template from sources; it enters as draft until tested.
- RV53. **Method transfers live in the full text, not in the abstract.** The transfer in a paper is stated in its methods or introduction ("we apply the z-transform to ..."), rarely in the abstract. The abstract-first screening of RV17 will therefore **miss them**. For the scout, stage 2 reads the **methods and introduction sections** of papers in the fields it targets (open access only, RV34), and the search can use "applied to" patterns ("... method ... applied to ... problem") besides keywords. This is a limit of the cheap path, and it makes the scout expensive; the cost is reported (RV48).

Test cases from the user's own field (told on 2026-10-21, **verified in the full texts the same day**, details and references in `RESEARCH_FINDINGS.md` F18, F19): (a) the **z-transform** from signal theory in Kunisch and Reiterer's Gautschi time-stepping approach to optimal control of the wave equation: Section 3.1 introduces vector valued signals "in analogy to ... signal processing", Appendix A defines their z-transform and expresses the discrete solution operator through the resolvent R(z); abstract and introduction do not mention it. (b) **Gröbner bases** from algebra in Bredies, Lorenz and Reiterer, "Minimization of non-smooth, non-convex functionals by iterative thresholding" (J. Optim. Theory Appl. 165(1), 2015; preprint SFB-Report 2014-015 of the University of Graz): Appendix A.2, "Tools from Algebraic Geometry", uses the theory of Gröbner bases to prove Proposition 5.1 (discreteness of quasi-global minimizers for rational p); the abstract does not mention it. Both are good less-famous cases for RV47, with two cautions: the models may have seen these papers, and the user is the author of both (no independent judgement of what counts as the transfer). **Both are in appendices**, which confirms RV53: a method transfer is rarely visible in an abstract.
  A third case, squaring the square (F17): Brooks, Smith, Stone and Tutte, 1940, with the blog logicpuzzles.ca/blog/squaring-the-square as source. A third source for the squaring-the-square case: logicpuzzles.ca/blog/squaring-the-square (read 2026-10-21): Brooks, Smith, Stone and Tutte, 1940, a squared rectangle as an electrical network (squares are wires, widths are currents, heights are voltage drops, Kirchhoff's current law); first perfect squared square by Sprague in 1939; smallest (21 squares, Duijvestijn 1978).

- RV48. **Place in the plan.** An optional role, off by default and switched on by the user per review ("look for methods from other fields"), built after the base review and the vocabulary bridging have numbers (phases). It uses the field graph (RV11: which fields are far away is exactly where transfers come from, so the scout reads **distant** fields, not only the related ones), the alias machinery (RV41) and the sandbox. Its cost is reported separately.

## 4. The agents (roles) (proposal, from the discussion)

- RV10. **Field experts**: one role per related sub-domain (for a question about electricity: electrical engineering as the main field, physics and mathematics as related). Each expert proposes keywords (RV16), judges the relevance of text pieces from its point of view (RV18) and fills its section of the report (RV27).
- RV11. **Field graph.** A stored graph of fields and subfields with weighted edges ("how relevant is B for a question in A", 0 to 1). The system analyser looks the question up in it; the number of experts is the main field plus the related fields above a relevance threshold, **at most N_max** (default 3 related, to be optimised, RV33). The graph starts from a public taxonomy (OpenAlex topics, to verify) plus hand edits, is stored in the library (4a) as data, and its weights are corrected from experience: a field whose expert never contributed a cited finding loses weight, one that did gains (RV35).
- RV12. **Main-field lead**: one role for the main field that gathers the experts' findings into the answer and resolves or flags conflicts.
- RV13. **Literature acquirer**: finds sources, keeps the references and the **relevance of each source with its reasons**, runs the search log, and owns the source table of the report. **Methodology specialist**: rates the *design* of each study (randomised trial, cohort, review, simulation, opinion, ...) and proposes how the evidence is to be weighed; together with the acquirer it writes the overall structure of the report (RV27).
- RV20. **Roles are views, not necessarily models.** With one language model loaded at a time (`OLLAMA_MAX_LOADED_MODELS=1`, one slot) a role is a prompt, a context view (the pieces and the graph neighbourhood the role sees, 13c CG3) and a schema, run by the same model one after the other. Different models per role are an option to test (diversity of errors), not an assumption: swapping models costs time, and the evidence for diversity from different models is not checked here. Light model for screening, full model for synthesis is the first split to try.
- RV21. A **critic** role (tries to refute a finding, 12.1 styles) is a variant to compare, not a default.
- RV44. **The number of domain experts is a setting, not a design (decided by the user, 2026-10-22).** It is not clear how much a team of experts helps in our workflows, so the comparison must be possible
  by changing one number. Fixed roles, always present: the **planner** (system analyser, component determiner and scheduler of RV1), the **collector** (the literature acquirer of RV13) and the
  **source expert** (the methodology and source specialist of RV13, which weighs the sources and their design). Adjustable: the **domain experts** of RV10: `domain_experts` = 0 means none (the fixed roles only,
  and one generalist judgement without a field view, the baseline of the comparison), 1 means the expert of the main field only, k means the main field and the k-1 most relevant related fields from the field graph
  (RV11); the setting `review_domain_experts` gives the default, a review request can override it, and the selection is by rule (relevance order) and can be overridden by a list of fields. The fixed roles do not
  depend on the number. Each role is, by setting, either a **view** of the one model (its own prompt, context view and schema) or **a model of its own** (`role_models`: role -> model name, default the same
  model for all), so that "views against separate models" is a comparison as well.
- RV45. **Comparisons are fair:** the benchmark of RV33 varies `domain_experts` (0, 1, 2, 3, 5) with the other settings fixed, and reports every variant twice: with the **same budget per expert** (more experts cost more) and
  with the **same total budget** (more experts get less each), plus the control of Hong and Page [5, 7] (N copies of one role against N different roles). Which roles took part, with which number, is part of the
  preparation of the session in the process graph (`REQUIREMENTS_CORE.md` PG11) and of the `env` of the run, so that a result can always be traced to its setting.

## 5. The process (proposal)

Each stage is a library pipeline of narrow nodes (4a), runs under the limits of RV30, and writes into the context graph (RV25).

- R0. **Intake and scope:** question, date cut-off, budget; one clarifying question if the question is ambiguous.
- R1. **Analyse** (system analyser): structure of the question, sub-questions, fields via the field graph, inclusion and exclusion criteria, the kind of evidence that would answer it. Output: plan as data (13b ST1) with targets.
- R2. **Components and keywords** (RV16): each expert proposes keywords; code merges, normalises and writes one search string per source type.
- R3. **Acquire:** scholarly APIs (OpenAlex, Semantic Scholar, arXiv, PubMed, Crossref; free, rate-limited, polite) and SearXNG for grey literature (reports, standards, official statistics); de-duplicate by DOI and title; metadata kept: date, venue, authors, citation count, open-access link, **retraction and correction status**. Only items **dated up to the cut-off** pass (RV32).
- R4. **Screen** in two stages:
  - RV17. *Stage 1, abstract first:* title, abstract and, where the full text is available, the conclusion; several expert votes (the ensemble form is the baseline) produce a relevance score; recall-oriented thresholds; borderline papers go to stage 2 instead of being dropped; a paper that is clearly off topic is dropped with its reason logged.
  - RV18. *Stage 2, pieces:* the full text (open access only, RV34) is cut into digestible pieces by code (sections, then paragraphs, RV2); the experts judge which pieces are relevant to which sub-question; the relevant ones are extracted **verbatim with an anchor check** (the quote must occur in the source, as in 9). The relevance of the source is then judged from the pieces.
- R5. **Weigh:** source credibility from the trust database (RV14), study design from the methodology specialist, recency; one weight per finding, computed by code from these parts so that it can be shown.
- R6. **Synthesise in the graph** (RV25): findings per sub-question, conflicts (13c CG5), open slots; the gap check names the open sub-questions and proposes further keywords; new sources found through references of good papers (one citation step, limited) and new keywords are added to the graph.
- R7. **Judge** the answerability (RV23) per sub-question and overall.
- R8. **Report** (RV27).

- RV19. **When to stop:** (a) a **sufficiency check by code** (every sub-question has anchored findings of enough weight, or has been searched with the keywords tried, RV23) plus the experts' vote as one signal; the model's feeling "answered" alone never ends the search (models tend to be sure of themselves, 12); (b) the source budget; (c) every **K sources checked (default 10, to be optimised, RV33)** MIA gives an **intermediate report** and asks whether to continue, with a recommendation and the reason ("two sub-questions are open; different keywords may help"). The user answering "no" ends the run with the report as it stands.
- RV22. **Sub-questions:** if the analysis or the gap check shows that the question must be split, the sub-questions get their own pass through R2 to R7 under the same budget (shared, not multiplied), as a child of the question in the graph.

## 6. Source trust (decided: a database of how much we trust sources) (proposal for the design)

- RV14. A **source trust table** in the library (4a, versioned, editable by the user): a prior trust 0 to 1 per **source class** (peer-reviewed journal, conference proceedings, preprint, thesis, official statistics or government body, standards body, textbook, quality press, trade press, company blog, personal blog, forum, wiki, unknown) and per **venue or domain** where known (journal quartile or indexing from OpenAlex/Scimago-like data, domain entries added by hand), each with its *reason* and the date it was set. Trust is a **judgement of ours**, shown as such; it is a **prior**, never a proof: the anchor check decides whether a quote is genuine, and a high-trust source can still be contradicted.
- RV15. **Source facts are read, not guessed:** type, venue, date, citation count and **retraction or correction status** (Crossref first, cross-checked) come from the metadata. A retracted paper is dropped from the evidence and listed in the report as retracted; one with an expression of concern is kept with the flag. Preprints are marked as not peer reviewed.
- RV40. **Fact check path (cheap):** one statement is searched; if a source with trust at or above a threshold states it (anchored), that is the answer and no model judges it; if the sources are low trust, silent or in conflict, it is escalated: a single model judges with the passages (12.1 styles), and a statement that needs more than a lookup becomes a review (RV3). Whether a single model or a simple comparison with a reputable source is enough is **measured on the research suites** (the existing `research` and `research_compound` suites are the baseline).

## 7. Keywords and the context graph (proposal)

- RV41. **Cross-field bridging.** Besides keywords, each expert states, for the concepts of the question, **what the concept is called in its own field** ("alias candidates") and which neighbouring fields might treat the same phenomenon. Alias candidates enter the graph as *proposed* `same_as` edges (13c CG2), never as facts. An alias becomes *accepted* only if (a) a retrieved source states the equivalence (a review, textbook or glossary that names both terms, anchored by quote), or (b) searching with it returns sources that the experts judge relevant (the yield test, logged). A term with no hits or only irrelevant hits is dropped and not asked again. Invented terms are the known risk of LLM expansion (section 3).
- RV42. **Keep the field of origin.** Every finding records the field whose vocabulary found it. The report shows findings that **only another field's vocabulary found** in their own part ("found under another name"), with the alias that led there, because that is the value this design is meant to add, and the place where STORM's over-association would show up (so those findings get the strictest relevance check, RV18).
- RV43a. **Normalise before matching** (code): transliteration and diacritics (Gröbner, Groebner, Grobner), hyphens and spaces, plural and singular, British and American spelling, common abbreviations. Learned in the first test: a query for "Groebner" did not match the text "Gröbner" of the paper that contained the transfer. Applies to searching, to the alias machinery (RV41) and to the quote anchor check.
- RV16. Keyword set per expert: synonyms, narrower and broader terms, abbreviations, the usual wording in that field. Merged by code; each keyword keeps who proposed it and what it found (number of hits, number kept). Keywords that found nothing useful are not repeated.
- RV25. **The review's context graph** (13c): nodes for the question, sub-questions, fields, keywords, sources, pieces (quotes), findings, conflicts, open slots; edges as in CG1 plus `proposed_by` (expert), `found_by` (keyword), `cites`. New sources and keywords found during reading are added; the graph decides what is read next (open slots first) and renders each model call's context within the budget (RV2). It is written to the run log; the graph viewer (10) can show how a finding came about.

## 8. Answerability in percent (decided: no verification, a judgement) (proposal for the definition)

- RV23. The figure is **computed by code from anchored evidence**, not asked from a model, with its parts shown: (1) *coverage:* share of sub-questions with at least one anchored finding of enough weight; (2) *weight:* the sum of finding weights (RV5 and R5), saturating; (3) *diversity:* independent sources (not sources that cite each other for the same claim); (4) *agreement:* penalty for unresolved conflicts, none for resolved ones; (5) *recency* where the question asks for the current state; (6) *search effort:* how many keywords and sources were tried. The experts' vote on "is the question answered" is shown next to it. The first formula is a transparent rubric with weights set by hand; it is **calibrated** on the benchmark (RV31): the figure is called a probability only if the benchmark shows that findings at 80 percent are right about 80 percent of the time (a calibration plot is part of the report of the evaluation).
- RV24. A **low figure is not an error.** The report says why it is low in one of a fixed set of causes: too little literature found, sources are weak or old, sources conflict, sub-questions are open, keywords probably wrong (with proposed alternatives). The user can start a follow-up run with these.

## 9. The report (proposal)

- RV27. Structure, set by the acquirer and the methodology specialist from the analysis, filled by the experts: question and scope; method (search strings, sources, date cut-off, screening counts like a PRISMA flow: found, de-duplicated, screened, kept, read); the sources table (reference, class, trust, design, relevance and why); findings per sub-question by field, each with its anchors; conflicts and how they were weighed; limitations; answerability with parts; open gaps and next keywords. Markdown, shown in the chat in short form (status block 5) and saved as a file in the run folder. **Every reference in the report must be a source the run actually retrieved** (hard check: it resolves to a stored DOI or URL and its quotes anchor); an invented reference is a failure by definition.
- The chat shows the playful line and the accurate step in brackets as in 5 (stage, counts, budget used).

## 10. Limits, budgets, side effects (decided)

- RV30. **Every run has hard limits** set before it starts and shown in the intermediate report: sources screened (stage 1), sources read (stage 2), model calls, tokens, wall time, search and API requests (polite rates, caching). Defaults small; the user can raise them for a run. Reaching a limit ends the stage and produces an intermediate report; nothing continues without the user (RV19c). A run is stoppable at any time; the report so far is kept.
- Side effects: scholarly APIs and web search are read-only; nothing is installed or executed from a source; text from papers is untrusted data (prompt injection, 11); the learning of RV35 only changes numbers in configuration and graph weights, never weights of models.

## 11. Evaluation: benchmarks for literature reviews (proposal)

- RV30a. **Time in slices (decided by the user, 2026-10-22).** A review starts with a first slice of **60 seconds**. When the slice (or any other limit) is used up, the run stops and gives an **intermediate report with a
  recommendation** (continue or stop, and why), and asks whether to go on; every "continue" adds one more slice (the size of the first by default; the user can name another, e.g. five minutes). The run never goes
  on by itself and never stops for good by itself. The recommendation comes from code, from what the last slice achieved: new relevant sources kept, new anchored findings, open slots closed, against what is still open. If the
  sufficiency check (RV19a) holds, the direction is clear and it recommends to stop; if the last slice brought something and gaps remain, it recommends to continue; a slice without progress recommends to stop or
  to change the keywords. This avoids both failures: a run that is stuck without anyone noticing, and a run that stops early or runs on although the direction is clear. The limits of RV30 stay as hard
  ceilings of a slice. (Built in `review_request.Budget.extend`; the recommendation comes with the pipeline.)
- RV31. **Reference set:** published literature reviews with a clear question and a known conclusion, of **varying outcomes** (supports, does not support, inconclusive), from 3 to 4 fields, open access where possible, including some of poor quality, outdated or contradicted by later work. Start with 10 to 20; candidates: parts of ScholarQABench, systematic reviews and meta-analyses whose included-study lists are public (abstract level when full text is not open). Split into a **development half** (tuning) and a **held-out half** (reported, never used for tuning).
- RV32a. **A cut-off in the past makes the metadata dishonest** (found while building phase 1): citation counts and retraction notices that the APIs return are as of today, not as of the cut-off. `scholar.use_as_of(until)` says so; a run with a cut-off in the past does not steer by citation counts, and retraction notices dated after the cut-off are ignored (`retraction_state(work, as_of)`). A work whose date is only a year counts as published on the last day of that year, and a work without a date is dropped when a cut-off is given.
- RV32. **Time cut-off:** for each reference review, only literature published **up to the review's search date** is allowed (the review itself and later papers are excluded by code). Limit that cannot be removed: the language model may have seen the review in training; reviews published after the model's cut-off date are preferred and the contamination risk is stated in every result.
- RV33. **Measures:** (a) *source recall and precision* against the reference review's included studies (at stage 1 and at the end); (b) *conclusion agreement* (direction and strength: supports, does not, inconclusive); (c) answerability calibration (RV23); (d) hallucinated references (must be 0); (e) quote anchoring (must be 100 percent); (f) calls, tokens, seconds. **Measures for H0:** *cross-field recall*: of the reference review's included studies, those that **do not contain the question's own key terms** (marked once by code from titles and abstracts) and the share of them found; compared for K0 keywords from a single model, K1 keywords from the main field only with synonyms, K2 keywords and aliases from several field experts (RV41). Plus precision of the aliases (accepted share, RV41), and the number of findings in the "found under another name" part that survive the relevance check (RV42). A second, small test is built by hand: concept pairs known to be the same in two fields, to see if the experts bring up the alias from the question alone. **Variants, same call budget where possible** so that cost is not confused with quality: A single model, one pass; B single model with ensemble screening and self-consistency on the conclusion (the Medprompt-like baseline); **C0 roles with different evidence, independent judgements, combined by weighted voting (no debate)**; C1 = C0 plus Society-of-Mind style rounds (1 to 2); D = C1 with agreement modulation (levels 0, 50, 90 percent) and with a belief-update bias toward correction; **control for diversity (Hong and Page): C0 with N copies of the same role against C0 with N different roles, same calls;** **the claim of H1 is D > C0 > B; C1 over C0 and D over C1 are each measured on their own**; E with and without the field graph (RV11); F with and without abstract-first (RV17); G the number of domain experts (0, 1, 2, 3, 5; RV44, RV45) and K varied. Repeats and the same statistics as 9; planners and debates are not deterministic, so stability is reported.
- **Guard against tuning into noise.** Debate is sensitive to settings, the reference set is small (10 to 20 reviews) and a run of a language model is not deterministic: tuning many settings on so few cases finds noise. So: few settings (rounds, agreement level, number of experts; at most 3 tuned), a fixed grid chosen before the first run, repeats per cell with the spread reported, tuning only on the development half and a single confirmation on the held-out half, and the result is reported as "better" only if the interval of the difference excludes zero. Also reported: tokens and seconds per review for every variant, because on one GPU with one slot the extra cost of debate is real time.
- When our conclusion **differs** from the reference, each case is classified: reference outdated (newer literature in the allowed window says otherwise), reference weak (low-quality studies, to be argued from the sources, not asserted), our miss (keywords, access, recall), our error (misreading, weighting). Only the last two count against the method; the first two are reported and may justify dropping a reference from the set (RV31), with the reason written down.
- RV34. Access: **full text from the start** (decided 2026-10-22: the conclusions matter more than the abstracts), open access only (links from the metadata, Unpaywall-like); a paywalled paper is used at abstract level and marked so.
  Reading order inside a paper: title, abstract, **conclusion**, then the rest as the question needs it (RV2 cuts it into pieces that fit). Formats in order of preference: structured text (arXiv HTML, PMC/JATS XML), HTML, PDF;
  a PDF is turned into text **inside the sandbox** (never in the main process; a PDF is untrusted input), within size and time limits. A local collection of the user's own PDFs is a later source (own material may use a pre-built graph, 13c CG7).

## 12. Reward and learning (decided as a direction; the design is a proposal)

The language models are fixed; what can improve is the **agent**: its configuration, thresholds, role choices and field-graph weights. This is the ladder of 8 of `REQUIREMENTS_CORE.md` applied here.

- RV35. **Level 1, fixed defaults** (everything works without learning). **Level 2, logged signals** for every run: the parts of RV23, calls, seconds, the user's reaction (kept, edited, rejected sections; "continue" or "stop"; a rating if given). **Level 3, a bandit over discrete choices** (number of experts, K, thresholds, agreement level, light or full model per stage, which keyword style), tried first **offline on the development half of the benchmark**, later online with user feedback, each change logged next to the default choice and reversible.
- RV36. **The reward functional** is a **vector, not one number**, and is never optimised alone: benchmark-side quality (source recall, conclusion agreement, calibration), cost (calls and seconds), the user's signals. A scalar for the bandit is made as a weighted sum with a cost term, the weights written down and changed only by the user. **Limits are constraints, not penalties** (RV30): a configuration cannot buy quality by exceeding a budget.
- RV37. **Guards against gaming the reward** (the usual failure of learned agents): (a) the figure being optimised is computed by code from anchored evidence, the model does not see it and cannot write it; (b) shrinking the search to look more certain is caught because coverage and search effort are parts of the figure and the benchmark measures source recall; (c) the held-out half is never used for tuning; (d) a learned choice has to beat the default on the development half *and* hold on the held-out half before it becomes the default; (e) every learned change is shown in the report of the run ("this run used 4 experts because ..."); (f) everything can be switched off.
- RV38. The **intermediate report and "continue?"** (RV19c) is also the main place where the user's signal enters, at no extra effort.
